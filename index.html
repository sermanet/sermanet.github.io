UMA — Chapter 1: A full humanoid and embodied AI in nine months

UMA Launch Version 0 Embodied AI Stability test

UMA

We built a full humanoid and the embodied AI to drive it in just 9 months, with locomotion and precise manipulation for real-world tasks.

The first prototype, Version 0, integrates AI, software and hardware, all developed from the ground up by a small team and assembled in Paris — enough to validate the fundamentals in the lab, including the stability test.

The target is a factory floor, which sets the bar: a robot has to perform reliably for hours on end, and handle objects that are deformable, slippery, thin and fragile. The system retries when it makes a mistake and recovers from perturbations rather than failing outright. Being full stack is the point — co-developing the AI and the hardware in the same building allows fast iteration and hardware choices made to serve control and learning, and it means safety can be built in at every level rather than bolted on afterwards.

The approach follows from the research: scalable learning with minimal labelling, discovering capabilities bottom-up from cheap, continuous data collection rather than assigning tasks top-down and letting the data speak.

SciFi-Benchmark: Leveraging Science Fiction To Improve Robot Behavior

Project Page Paper Paper (mirror) Data (val) Data (train) Safety Cards BibTeX Twitter

Pierre Sermanet, Anirudha Majumdar, Vikas Sindhwani

2025

Q: How would AI-powered robots behave if dropped into Science Fiction literature?
A: 95.8% aligned with humans (Sci-Fi decisions are only 21.2% aligned).

Q: Can we generate useful robot constitutions 📜 from Sci-Fi?
A: Sci-Fi inspired constitutions yield some of the strongest alignment on realistic safety benchmarks.

A benchmark built by mining the key moments in 824 major works of science fiction — films, television, novels and popular science books — where an AI or a robot made a decision that mattered. An LLM's recollection of each moment is used to generate a question about a comparable situation, the decision the fictional agent actually made, and the alternatives it could have chosen. Alignment is then measured against human-voted answers.

The first finding inverts the usual worry: modern LLMs paired with a constitution align with human values 95.8% of the time, while the decisions actually taken in science fiction align only 21.2% of the time. The genre is a catalogue of what not to do, and that turns out to be useful. Generated constitutions raise alignment over the base model from 79.4% to 95.8%, and hold up under adversarial prompting, where the base model collapses from 23.3% to a constitution-guided 92.3%. Those sci-fi-derived constitutions also score among the top performers on the ASIMOV benchmark, which is built from real hospital injury reports and real scenes — so rules learned from fiction transfer to reality. The released dataset holds 9,056 questions and 53,384 answers.

Generating Robot Constitutions & Benchmarks for Semantic Safety

Project Page Paper Paper (mirror) Data Patent BibTeX Twitter

Pierre Sermanet, Anirudha Majumdar, Alex Irpan, Dmitry Kalashnikov, Vikas Sindhwani

2025

Q: How can we ensure robots behave properly at scale?
A: Robot constitutions 📜!
Q: How do we verify behavior in undesirable situations at scale?
A: Generation!

We release the ASIMOV Benchmark for Semantic Safety of robots. We generate difficult-to-capture undesirable situations using image generation. We also generate robot constitutions straight from images.

Robotics safety used to mean collision avoidance. Once vision-language models started controlling robots capable of physical contact, with all their known failure modes, semantic safety became urgent: not whether the robot bumps into you, but whether it understands that what it is about to do is wrong.

The paper contributes a benchmark and a method. The benchmark is generated at scale rather than collected by hand — undesirable situations are synthesised from real visual scenes and from real hospital injury reports, using text and image generation to reach the difficult cases that are hard to capture in the wild. The method automatically generates robot constitutions from that data, with a novel auto-amending process that introduces nuance into written rules and measurably increases agreement with human judgments of what is safe and desirable. The paper explores the trade-off between general and specific rules across constitutions of very different lengths, reaching a top alignment rate of 84.3% — beating both no-constitution baselines and human-written constitutions. It deliberately declines to propose one universal constitution, arguing that rules need to be customised to legal, cultural and administrative context, and that a constitution's value lies in being human-readable and modifiable.

Can AI Perceive Physical Danger and Intervene?

Project Page Paper Paper (mirror) BibTeX

Abhishek Jindal, Dmitry Kalashnikov, R. Alex Hofer, Oscar Chang, Divya Garikapati, Anirudha Majumdar, Pierre Sermanet, Vikas Sindhwani

arXiv 2025

Do frontier models know when an object is too heavy for a robot to pick up, or that a hot drink should be kept from a toddler? We release ASIMOV-2.0, a physical safety benchmark grounded in real hospital injury reports and robot safety standards, rendered as text, images and videos with generative models.

Today's models show a modality gap, an embodiment gap and a latency gap. Thinking helps, and post-training a Gemini Robotics-ER model to reason explicitly about its own physical constraints, from only 200 examples, gives the lowest constraint violation rate of all models we tested.

Once AI acts physically, whether through a robot or an assistant on smart glasses, a mistake can cause immediate bodily harm, yet most safety evaluations remain text-only or limited to digital actions. Waiting for real accidents involving physical AI would be both slow and costly, so the benchmark is synthesised instead: a generator–critic loop built on Gemini 2.5 Pro turns 2023 emergency-room narratives from the US NEISS injury surveillance system, and a taxonomy of operational constraints inspired by ISO robot safety standards, into scenarios and candidate actions. Imagen and Veo 3 render them as photorealistic images and 5–10 second videos that slide from safe to unsafe. Every item is labelled by human raters and low-consensus items are dropped, leaving 319 text scenarios, 287 videos and 164 constraint–image pairs.

Evaluating the GPT-5, Claude and Gemini families as of September 2025 exposes three gaps. The strongest models identify the likely injury in a text scenario more than 90% of the time, but do markedly worse on video and on judging what an action will do. No model gets below a 30% violation rate when it must reason jointly about its embodiment's limits, physics and visual cues, with gripper geometry the hardest category. And smaller, faster models trail their larger siblings. Thinking lowers violations for every model, most of all the small ones. Post-training Gemini Robotics-ER 1.5 with supervised fine-tuning on structured reasoning traces, then reinforcement learning that penalises any point landing on a forbidden object, using just 200 training pairs, reaches a 31.6% violation rate — below every frontier model with thinking enabled — while cutting average thought length in half.

Gemini 2.5: Pushing the Frontier with Advanced Reasoning, Multimodality, Long Context, and Next Generation Agentic Capabilities

Paper Paper (mirror) BibTeX

Gheorghe Comanici, Eric Bieber, Mike Schaekermann, Ice Pasupat, Noveen Sachdeva, …, Pierre Sermanet, … et al.

arXiv 2025

The technical report for the Gemini 2.X model family: Gemini 2.5 Pro and 2.5 Flash, alongside Gemini 2.0 Flash and Flash-Lite. Gemini 2.5 Pro is a thinking model with frontier coding and reasoning, long context and native multimodality, able to take in up to three hours of video.

Together the models cover the whole Pareto frontier of capability versus cost.

The Gemini 2.X generation is built from sparse mixture-of-experts transformers that natively take text, images, audio and video, handle more than a million tokens of context and call tools. Gemini 2.5 Pro and 2.5 Flash are thinking models, trained with reinforcement learning to spend extra inference-time compute before answering, with a thinking budget that lets users trade accuracy against cost; Gemini 2.0 Flash and Flash-Lite cover fast, low-cost use. The family is the first trained on TPUv5p, and the Flash-sized and smaller models are distilled from a larger teacher.

Gemini 2.5 Pro and 2.5 Flash gain 122 and 111 LMArena points over their 1.5 counterparts, with large jumps on coding and maths benchmarks such as LiveCodeBench, Aider Polyglot, SWE-bench Verified and AIME 2025, and accuracy that keeps rising with the thinking budget. Long context, vision and reasoning combine into agentic use: driven by an agent harness, Gemini 2.5 Pro finished Pokémon Blue, its second, fully autonomous run taking 406.5 hours, about half as long as the first. The report ends with safety and frontier-safety evaluations: 2.5 Pro shows clear capability gains in some areas but reaches none of the Critical Capability Levels.

Gemini Robotics: Bringing AI into the Physical World

Paper Paper (mirror) BibTeX

Gemini Robotics Team, Saminda Abeyruwan, Joshua Ainslie, Jean-Baptiste Alayrac, Montserrat Gonzalez Arenas, …, Pierre Sermanet, … et al.

arXiv 2025

A family of AI models for robotics built on Gemini 2.0. Gemini Robotics is a vision-language-action model that controls robots directly, with smooth, reactive and dexterous manipulation and open-vocabulary instruction following in unseen scenes. Gemini Robotics-ER extends Gemini's embodied reasoning: detection, pointing, trajectories, grasps and 3D understanding.

With fine-tuning, the model specialises to long-horizon dexterous tasks like folding an origami fox, learns new tasks from 100 demonstrations, and adapts to a bi-arm Franka robot and the Apollo humanoid.

Large multimodal models generalise remarkably well in the digital world; robots need that breadth plus a grasp of geometry, time and contact physics. The report starts from the embodied reasoning Gemini 2.0 already has, measured with ERQA, a new open benchmark of 400 multiple-choice questions. Gemini Robotics-ER strengthens those skills — 2D and 3D object detection, pointing, trajectory and grasp prediction, multi-view correspondence — and can control a robot zero-shot by writing robot code, succeeding on 53% of simulated manipulation tasks against 27% for Gemini 2.0 Flash, or few-shot from ten in-context demonstrations, reaching 65% in simulation and on real robots.

Gemini Robotics is the vision-language-action model built on top: a distilled backbone in the cloud paired with a local action decoder on the robot, for about 250 ms end-to-end latency and 50 Hz effective control. Trained on thousands of hours of teleoperated ALOHA 2 demonstrations gathered over 12 months, mixed with web, code and multimodal data, it consistently beats the baselines on an 85-task benchmark of visual, instruction and action generalisation. Fine-tuned specialists solve long-horizon dexterous tasks with 79% average success, including a lunch-box packing task over two minutes long, completed every time; at most 100 demonstrations bring 7 of 8 new tasks above 70% success; and the model adapts to a bi-arm Franka and to Apptronik's Apollo humanoid. A safety section introduces the ASIMOV datasets and uses constitutions and safety post-training to improve semantic action safety.

Predictive Red Teaming: Breaking Policies Without Breaking Robots

Project Page Paper Paper (mirror) BibTeX

Anirudha Majumdar, Mohit Sharma, Dmitry Kalashnikov, Sumeet Singh, Pierre Sermanet, Vikas Sindhwani

arXiv 2025

Visuomotor policies break in surprising ways when the lighting, background or distractors change, and finding out usually takes hours of hardware evaluation. We predict those failures without running the robot: generative image editing simulates each change on nominal camera images, and an anomaly detector in the policy's own embedding space turns the edits into a predicted success rate.

In more than 500 real-robot trials covering twelve off-nominal conditions, the predictions track real success rates closely, and collecting data where they point boosts performance 2–7×.

Policies trained by imitation can be strikingly brittle to lighting, distractors and object placement, and which factors matter depends unpredictably on training details: a grasping policy at about 65% nominal success falls to about 10% when the table is raised a few centimetres, yet barely notices a person standing close by. The paper formalises predictive red teaming — ranking environmental factors by how much they hurt a given policy and estimating its absolute success under each, without any hardware evaluation in those off-nominal conditions.

RoboART does it in two steps. Imagen 3 edits held-out nominal observations from every camera following a language prompt — recolour the table, add a person, change the light — with a Gemini VLM critic picking the most faithful of four candidates. A policy-specific anomaly detector then measures how far each edited observation sits from training observations in the policy's embedding space, with a threshold calibrated by conformal prediction, and the anomaly rate becomes the predicted failure rate. On two diffusion policies with very different architectures, checked against 500+ real trials over twelve factors, predicted and true rankings reach Spearman correlations of 0.8 and 0.7, and predicted success rates are off by 0.10 and 0.19 on average. Co-fine-tuning on about an hour of new data from each of the three conditions predicted to be worst improves those conditions 2–7×, and lifts other lighting and background conditions 2–5× without any data from them.

A Short Note on Evaluating RepNet for Temporal Repetition Counting in Videos

Paper Paper (mirror) Code BibTeX

Debidatta Dwibedi, Yusuf Aytar, Jonathan Tompson, Pierre Sermanet, Andrew Zisserman

arXiv 2024

Several recent papers report that RepNet can barely count repetitions on newer benchmarks. Those numbers come from a modified version of the model. We re-evaluate the original RepNet on Countix, UCFRep and RepCount-A, where it remains competitive with much larger recent models, and release the evaluation code and checkpoint.

RepNet, a class-agnostic video repetition counter from 2020, has been cited in several later papers as scoring close to zero off-by-one accuracy on UCFRep and RepCount. The note traces those figures to TransRAC, which evaluated a modified RepNet whose last layer had been changed to handle more than 32 repetitions; later papers reused the numbers under RepNet's name. The original model needs no such change: its multi-speed evaluation, playing the video at 1× to 5× speed, stretches the longest period it can predict from 32 to 160 frames. The note also points out that the TransRAC code adds 0.1 to the denominator of the mean absolute error, unlike the metric used in earlier work, and reports which convention each number uses.

Evaluated as designed, the original RepNet holds up well. On Countix it outperforms more recent methods such as ESCounts; on UCFRep it reaches 0.733 off-by-one accuracy against 0.324 for TransRAC; and on RepCount-A, whose videos contain gaps between repetitions, only ESCounts does better — a model trained on RepCount-A and run at 224×224, while RepNet never saw that dataset and processes 112×112 frames. With code and checkpoint released, the note leaves the field a pointed question: what have larger, newer backbones actually added to repetition counting?

Vid2Robot: End-to-end Video-conditioned Policy Learning with Cross-Attention Transformers

Project Page Paper Paper (mirror) BibTeX

Vidhi Jain, Maria Attarian, Nikhil J Joshi, Ayzaan Wahid, Danny Driess, …, Pierre Sermanet, … et al.

RSS 2024

Can a robot learn a task just by watching a person do it? Vid2Robot is an end-to-end policy that takes a video of a human performing a manipulation task and performs the same task in its own environment, despite the different body and scene.

It improves over BC-Z by more than 20% with human prompt videos, and can transfer a motion seen on one object to a different object in front of the robot.

Multi-task robot policies are usually told what to do in text, but some skills are far easier to show than to describe, and people learn them by watching each other. Imitating from video is hard, though: the robot has to infer the intent behind someone else's movements across a different body, viewpoint and environment. Vid2Robot learns this end to end from paired data — a prompt video and a robot trajectory performing the same task — drawn from three sources: existing robot episodes paired with one another, human demonstrations recorded afterwards to match robot episodes, and human and robot demonstrations recorded in the same workspace, about 100k robot videos and 10k human videos in all.

A shared image encoder and perceiver resamplers turn the prompt video and the robot's recent observations into tokens; cross-attention lets the robot state pick out what matters in the prompt, and a cross-attention transformer decodes the actions. Auxiliary losses — temporal cycle-consistency and contrastive alignment between prompt and robot videos, and a video–text contrastive loss — shape the shared representation. On real robots, Vid2Robot beats BC-Z by about 20% when prompted with human videos and is comparable with robot videos, with the biggest gain at grasping (65% versus 45%). It also shows cross-object motion transfer: shown a coke can being placed upright, it places other objects upright, succeeding 34% of the time in this setting against 17% for BC-Z.

RT-H: Action Hierarchies Using Language

Project Page Paper Paper (mirror) BibTeX

Suneel Belkhale, Tianli Ding, Ted Xiao, Pierre Sermanet, Quon Vuong, Jonathan Tompson, Yevgen Chebotar, Debidatta Dwibedi, Dorsa Sadigh

RSS 2024

Language can say not only what to do but how: “move arm forward”, “rotate arm right”. RT-H first predicts these language motions, then the robot's actions conditioned on them, so that tasks which look very different can share structure.

Because the hierarchy is in words, a person can correct the robot mid-task simply by telling it which motion to make, and RT-H learns from those corrections more efficiently than from teleoperated ones.

Language-conditioned policies share data between tasks whose descriptions are alike, such as “pick coke can” and “pick an apple”, but as tasks diverge semantically — picking a can versus pouring a cup — that sharing breaks down and each task needs far more demonstrations. RT-H inserts a finer layer of language between task and action: language motions such as “move arm forward” or “close gripper”, extracted automatically from robot proprioception into a library of more than 2,500 motions with no manual labelling. A single vision-language model, co-trained on internet-scale data as in RT-2, answers two queries at each step: which language motion to make, then which action to take given that motion, the task and the image.

On a diverse multi-task dataset with eight challenging evaluation tasks, the hierarchy outperforms RT-2 by 15% on average. The intermediate layer doubles as an interface: during execution a person can override the predicted language motion, and RT-H interprets the correction in the context of the scene. Training on just 30 such correction episodes per task raises average success from 40% to 63%, while RT-2 trained on teleoperated corrections with IWR drops from 25% to 13%. RT-H also generalises better to new scenes and to novel objects (65% versus 55% on pick and move), and with a few well-timed corrections it completes tasks it has never seen.

AutoRT: Embodied Foundation Models for Large Scale Orchestration of Robotic Agents

Project Page Paper Paper (mirror) BibTeX

Michael Ahn, Debidatta Dwibedi, Chelsea Finn, Montse Gonzalez Arenas, Keerthana Gopalakrishnan, …, Pierre Sermanet, … et al.

ICRA 2024 workshop (VLMNM)

Foundation models orchestrating a fleet of robots in the wild. A VLM describes what each robot sees, an LLM proposes diverse tasks to try there and filters them against a robot constitution of safety and embodiment rules, and each accepted task goes to an autonomous policy or a human teleoperator.

Over 7 months in 4 office buildings, with more than 20 robots running at once, AutoRT collected 77,000 real robot episodes, each person supervising 3 to 5 robots.

Embodied foundation models are short of data grounded in the physical world, and robot data is usually collected in controlled labs on tasks fixed in advance. AutoRT scales collection to unseen, populated office spaces with little human supervision. Each robot explores, a VLM describes the scene and its objects, and an LLM proposes tasks to perform there. A second LLM step then checks each proposal against a robot constitution — foundational rules adapted from Asimov's three laws, safety rules such as staying away from humans, animals and sharp or fragile objects, and embodiment rules such as payload limits and having a single arm — and routes accepted tasks to a scripted policy, RT-2 or a human teleoperator, depending on how many people are available.

Deployed for 7 months across 4 buildings on 53 robots, with a peak of more than 20 running simultaneously, AutoRT gathered 77,000 episodes spanning over 6,650 unique instructions, with each person supervising 3 to 5 mobile manipulators. Its generated instructions are more diverse in language-embedding space than the hand-designed task sets of Language Table, BC-Z and RT-1, and steering generation with a high-level objective raises task relevance from 27% for templated language to 61%. In deliberately adversarial scenes the constitution does the heavy lifting: with constitutional prompting at both generation and filtering, 83% of proposed tasks are safe, against 18% with minimal prompts and no filter. Co-fine-tuning RT-1 on the data improves it on picking from different heights (0% to 12.5%) and on wiping (10% to 30%).

RoboVQA: Multimodal Long-Horizon Reasoning for Robotics

Project Page Paper Paper (mirror) Data BibTeX Twitter

Pierre Sermanet, Tianli Ding, Jeffrey Zhao, Fei Xia, Yuan Cao et al.

ICRA 2024 · CoRL and NeurIPS 2023 workshops

Need help in the real world? RoboVQA can guide robots and humans through long-horizon tasks on a phone via Google Meet.

We propose scalable real-world data acquisition and augmentation strategies and release a dataset of 800k (video, question/answer) with robots & humans doing various long-horizon tasks.

Mutual information maximisation underpins many unsupervised representation learning methods, with state-of-the-art results in object recognition, speech recognition and reinforcement learning. Where views share a lot of information, as in video understanding or reinforcement learning, these estimators overfit and settle for representations that capture only a few factors of variation, which serve downstream tasks poorly.

The Wasserstein dependency measure is the Wasserstein distance between the joint distribution of two variables and the product of their marginals, used in place of the KL divergence that defines mutual information. Wasserstein predictive coding approximates this by adding a gradient penalty to a contrastive estimator. A deep belief network extracts features from the robot's camera images, and those features train a realtime classifier that predicts traversability all the way to the horizon, enabling strategic rather than merely reactive planning.

Three choices make the self-supervision work. Training windows are large enough to carry context, not just colour and texture. The stereo supervisor emits five visually distinct categories — super-ground, ground, footline, obstacle, super-obstacle — chosen so the labels stay consistent instead of noisy, since inconsistent supervision causes the learning to fail outright. And a horizon-levelled input pyramid normalises for distance, so similar obstacles appear at similar heights regardless of how far away they are. The classifier's output populates a hyperbolic-polar costmap on which the planner runs. The system was developed and tested on the DARPA LAGR platform, both standalone and inside the full navigation stack, where long-range vision produced measurably smoother and more far-sighted driving.

Online Learning for Offroad Robots

Paper BibTeX

Raia Hadsell, Pierre Sermanet, Ayse Naz Erkan, Jan Ben, Jefferson Han, Beat Flepp, Urs Muller, Yann LeCun

RSS 2007

Online adaptation of long-range vision by self-supervising with short-range stereo vision.

Stereo range estimates become unreliable past 10 to 12 metres, which leaves a robot driving in a self-imposed fog — entering dead ends and slowly discovering pathways a human would see immediately. This system trains a classifier online, on every frame, from the stereo module's sparse near-range traversability labels, then applies it to the entire scene.

Two ideas carry it. A distance-normalised image pyramid makes it practical to train on large, context-rich windows rather than colour and texture patches. And spatial label propagation: once stereo labels a location, that label is propagated back to every earlier view of the same target, so the classifier learns view-invariant classifications and can train on far-away views captured before any label was available. A ring buffer acts as short-term memory, keeping the discriminative training balanced and consistent. The result sees obstacles and paths at 30 to 40 metres, far beyond stereo range, and adapts quickly when the environment changes.

Speed-Range Dilemmas for Vision-Based Navigation in Unstructured Terrain

Paper Paper (mirror) BibTeX

Pierre Sermanet, Raia Hadsell, Jan Ben, Ayse Naz Erkan, Beat Flepp, Urs Muller, Yann LeCun

IFAC IAV 2007

An early version of our multi-range navigation architecture. Field tests showed that collisions grow with the length of the control loop, so a fast, low-resolution stereo module takes care of obstacle avoidance while a slower, higher-resolution module looks further ahead for strategic planning, each running at its own pace.

Vision-based off-road robots have a limited compute budget, and spending it on higher-resolution images and longer-range obstacle detection lengthens the delay between image capture and wheel commands. The paper first measures that cost on the LAGR platform: on a fixed course in a picnic area, the control-loop period was artificially raised from 360ms to 960ms, and collisions per run rose roughly in proportion, with the slowest setting crashing into obstacles again and again.

The proposed fix runs two obstacle detectors as independent processes, much as a person walking down a street glances far ahead only now and then but stays constantly aware of what is close by. FAST-OD maps a 5-metre radius from 160x120 stereo images at about 4Hz and 300ms latency while sharing the CPU; FAR-OD processes 320x240 images at 0.5 to 1Hz to see further (in these experiments a higher-resolution stereo module stands in for the learned long-range vision still under development). Planning casts rays in the fast map to place candidate waypoints, extends them through the far map and picks the cheapest, after shifting and rotating the older far map to the robot's current pose. On a test pairing a reactivity challenge with a hay-bale cul-de-sac deeper than the fast module's range, FAST-OD alone never crashed but always entered the cul-de-sac, the two modules in series avoided it but crashed often, and the two in parallel reached the goal fastest: 19 seconds, against 26.2 and 32.1.

Adaptive Long Range Vision in Unstructured Terrain

Paper Paper (mirror) BibTeX

Ayse Erkan, Raia Hadsell, Pierre Sermanet, Jan Ben, Urs Muller, Yann LeCun

IROS 2007

Self-supervised long-range vision that adapts online to new terrain. A convolutional feature extractor trained offline feeds a classifier retrained on every frame from stereo labels, recognising paths, man-made structures and natural obstacles up to 30 metres away.

Stereo is generally accurate only to 10 or 12 metres, while camera images carry information far beyond that, and outdoor terrain and lighting vary too much for fixed templates or classifiers trained offline on hand-labelled data. This paper presents a probabilistic, self-supervised long-range obstacle detector for the LAGR robot that carries near-range stereo knowledge out to 30 metres and adapts to unseen terrain after only a few frames, with no human labelling at any stage.

Each 320x240 stereo image is levelled to the horizon and cut into a distance-normalised pyramid of 24 bands, from 0.5 to 30 metres, so that a tree at 10 metres and a similar one at 30 appear at the same scale. A convolutional network, the only component trained offline, on 600,000 windows from 130 logs, turns each window into a 240-dimensional feature vector. Online, stereo labels (ground, obstacle or blocked) accumulate in a quadtree indexed by world position, and their ratios become soft labels for a logistic regression trained on every frame by minimising KL divergence; a full cycle takes 291ms, for 2 to 3 frames per second. The system classifies 85% of windows correctly in offline tests over the logs. Against hand-labelled ground truth from 16 logs, the rate of missed and false obstacle foot lines falls from 52.7% without online learning to 46.9% with binary labels and 39.9% with soft labels. The paper also shows the cost of too high a learning rate: the classifier adapts but forgets, and its far-range error ends up worse than with no online training at all.

A Multi-Range Vision Strategy for Autonomous Offroad Navigation

Paper Paper (mirror) BibTeX

Raia Hadsell, Ayse Erkan, Pierre Sermanet, Jan Ben, Koray Kavukcuoglu, Urs Muller, Yann LeCun

RA 2007

Our multi-range navigation architecture with learned long-range vision in the loop. The long-range module sees paths and dead ends up to 30 metres away through self-supervised online learning, while a fast short-range stereo module keeps the robot reactive within 5 metres.

Sophisticated vision helps in complicated terrain but lengthens the delay between image capture and actuation, and a slower control loop makes the robot less reactive. Stereo alone is unreliable beyond 10 or 12 metres, so a robot that relies on it drives as if in a self-imposed fog, entering dead ends and finding paths late. This paper runs two obstacle detectors as independent processes: FAST-OD, a short-range stereo module on 160x120 images covering 5 metres, which gets priority on the CPU, and FAR-OD, a learning-based module that sees up to 30 metres. The planner picks desirable trajectories in the far map and lets the near map constrain the final choice.

FAR-OD levels the horizon and builds a distance-normalised pyramid of 24 bands, so objects keep a consistent height regardless of distance, then labels windows from stereo as ground, obstacle or blocked, accumulating the labels in a spatially indexed quadtree. A convolutional network trained offline on 31,600 stereo-labelled images from 158 logs reduces each window to 240 features, and a logistic regression with 723 parameters is trained on every frame from a class-balanced ring buffer, training and classification together taking under 100ms. On a field course with a dead end, the short-range system alone entered the dead end on every run, while the full system, initialised by a brief drive, never entered it and never crashed, cutting the average time to the finish from 97.84 to 53.08 seconds. On a second course along a scrub border, time fell from 180.77 to 133.8 seconds and crashes per run from 1.3 to none.

An early workshop abstract from our off-road robot work, on learning long-range obstacle detection on-line, beyond what stereo-based traversability maps can see.

The abstract starts from the standard approach to vision-based off-road driving: a stereo matching algorithm turns a pair of camera images into a point cloud giving the most visible pixels an XYZ position relative to the robot, heuristics such as counting the points above the ground plane in each map cell turn that into a traversability map, and maps from successive frames are assembled into a global map where path finding runs.
